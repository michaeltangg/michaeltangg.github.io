ASIC Reverse-Engineering Puzzle

Intro

The goal of this puzzle, created by Jane Street, is to extract the functionality of a circuit by reverse engineering the circuit's layout. Provided is the final mask of the circuit (puzzle.gds) along with a waveform of example inputs and their corresponding outputs (example_inputs.vcd). None of the provided examples flip the [success] output high, meaning the logic driving [success] has to be recovered from the layout itself.

Also included is an annotated hint image (layout.png) marking a region of the design as an "output generator". The output generator is hinted to not be necessary when reverse engineering the core logic, but will eventually be needed to retrieve the hidden message. This splits the problem into two mostly-independent halves: first recover the condition that drives [success] high, then figure out what [out] produces once it does.

Warmup

Included alongside the main puzzle is a scaled down warm-up puzzle aimed to help us acclimate to the tools and environment we will be using. The warm-up includes the source RTL in Verilog, a synthesized netlist, a placed-and-routed DEF, and the final layout. Using these as ground truth, we can build a set of general-purpose scripts to reverse engineer the puzzle and verify each technique against the warm-up before trusting it on the real thing. The pipeline has three stages:

1. Extract a gate-level netlist from the raw GDS layout.

KLayout allows us to import the raw GDS file and view the different metals layers and cells that comprise the circuit. While helpful in understanding the connectivity between [success] logic and the output generator, we are more focused on Klayout's LayoutToNetlist tool that treats standard cells as black boxes and extracts the netlist purely from routing metals and pin labels.

2. Validate the netlist against the warm-up RTL/DEF.

Since the warmup already includes the layout's associated netlist and RTL, we can verify LayoutToNetlist against that ground truth. The extracted per-instance celltype, placement, and pin→net are matched bijectively against DEF (celltype, x, y) → instname, and netlist instname → {pin:netname} instances. Exact set-equality between the extracted and reference netlist proves our structural isomorphism.

Behavioral isomorphism is verified using Icarus Verilog's iverilog + vvp by driving identical inputs into both DUTs side by side. After each posedge + 1 settle, S_reference !== S_extracted is checked to prove the extracted netlist is behaviorally identical.

3. Reverse-trace the gate-level logic to build up the driving condition.

The verified netlist is passed through Yosys ABC to collapse the Skywater130 cells into logic primitives (AND/OR/XOR/NOT/MUX/DFF). This simplifies the topology-walking logic to avoid stdcell library special cases. For each flop, we walk backwards from the D input pin through combinational cells only, stopping at the first flop or primary input we hit, giving us a per-flop dependency map.

In the warmup flop dependency map, we find that all flops have at most one predecessor flop that feeds into it. Furthermore, when we start the walk at the chain heads (flops whose D input depends on a top-level primary and no other flop), we find exactly 2 chains, each having a length of 8. From this, it is implied that the logical structure of the circuit is comprised of two 8-bit shift registers. To find the actual driving condition of S, we exhaustively enumerate through all 256 × 256 = 65536 possible (a, b) pairs where a and b are two 8-bit integers to find S = 1 when a + b == 496. Exhaustive search is used here because of the relatively small state space. For the actual puzzle, that approach wouldn't be practical and a different approach will be needed.

Moving to the real puzzle

For the real puzzle, we don't have the same ground truth RTL or netlists provided, with only example_inputs.vcd as a reference to verify our extracted netlist is accurate. Similar to the approach in the warmup puzzle, the goal is to verify that our extracted netlist is behaviorally identical to the example inputs/outputs provided. We instantiate the top module of extracted_puzzle.v and replay the same recorded input sequence and check that every input signal change becomes a <signal> = <value>; assignment at the matching delay to confirm this. While we are able to confirm that the extraction is at the very least identical in behavior to the provided input, success never toggles high in any of the example inputs, requiring us to continue working on the netlist's structure directly.

Netlist algebra: recovering the logic

Again, similar to the warmup pipeline, we reverse-trace the flop-dependency from the success output. There are slight differences between the walk in the warmup and the walk in the puzzle. In the puzzle, we are operating directly on the extracted puzzle from LayoutToNetlist instead of passing the netlist through Yosys ABC. The abc-resynthesized JSON introduced a "clk as data" artifact where the clock signal was folded into combinational logic (net200 = net193 AND clk) which is not found in the raw netlist design. The original benefit of using Yosys was the ability to collapse ~66 distinct cell types down to 5 uniform primitives along with the ability to use built-in tools like yosys-smtbmc. We are able to avoid this artifact by walking the raw netlist, but this comes at the cost of increasing complexity when we start analyzing. Since the warmup was a synchronous design that never crosses a time-domain, the synthesized netlist is acyclic by construction. The puzzle's success cone is instead 56 flops deep with heavy reconvergent fan-in, so each walk carries a per-flop visited set and hard recursion cap to avoid re-traversing shared subgraphs exponentially.

Since extraction scrubs all the names in the design, the topology of the circuit becomes ambiguous. For example, a chain of flops in the dependency graph could either be a shift register or a ripple counter since the distinction lies in the combinational logic between the stages. The walk was sufficient in describing the connectivity of the circuit, but not enough to describe the individual function of each flop. To fix this, we observe the internal state directly. The sky130 cell models generated from the liberty hold flop state in an internal reg IQ, so a testbench can reach a flop's state hierarchically by instance name (dut._inst_13_.IQ). We use iverilog to instantiate the extracted netlist against the cell models, drive rst_n/enable/I and print the selected flops' state on every cycle.

Using these flop states, we uncover several logical structures that comprise the circuit. The entire circuit is comprised of 92 flops. Analyzing the flop dependency graph further, we find a minimal closed subgraph of 9 flops: flops whose behavior is fully determined by themselves plus clk/enable/rst_n. Additionally, these 9 flops comprise the "core" of our design as every single other flop depends on these 9 flops. All together, the 92 flops are made up of 9 core, 69 data, 13 output generator, and the final success flop.

Watching the switching behavior of the 9 core flops, we find a two-digit base-11 counter with 121 states. These 9 flops are split into the 4 low digits, 4 high digits, and a sticky latch. The core's state starts at cycle 0: low={0,0,0,1} high={0,0,0,0} hub=0 and increments until cycle 119: low={1,0,1,0} high={1,0,1,0} hub=0. Cycle 119 represents (10,10), which is 120 in base 11, the counter's terminal state. On cycle 120: low={0,0,0,0} high={0,0,0,0} hub=1, the counter has wrapped back to zero and the latch has fired, freezing it there for the rest of the run and giving us our 121-state counter.

Having reverse-engineered the "core" logic, we turn to the other 69 flops. Watching those flops with respect to the 121-state counter, we find within those 69 flops: 11 "keys" with each key having a 2-bit counter that saturates at 3. The 11 keys correspond with the cycle i mod 11, each key being visited 11 times: key 4 comes around at cycles 4, 15, 26, 37, 48, 59, 70, 81, 92, 103, 114. Each time the counter points at a key, input I is sampled. If I is high on that cycle, the key is "pressed", incrementing the 2-bit counter by 1. If I is low, nothing happens to the counter. If both MSB and LSB of the counter are high, the counter is saturated and freezes instead of rolling back to 0.

success's direct fan-in consists of 57 flops: success itself, hub, _inst_51_, and the 54 flops that make up the acceptance condition. _inst_51_ exists as a one-cycle-delayed sticky copy of hub and exists to keep the self-hold term alive. The 54 flops comprising the acceptance condition break down as 2 sets of 11 2-bit saturating counters, an 8-bit binary counter of total key presses, and 2 extra flops. Of the remaining 14 of the 69 flops, 12 form a shift register with the last 2 settling to a fixed state regardless of input. At this point in the puzzle, we have uncovered the full structure of the circuit, but only understand the function of one set of 11 2-bit saturating counters and the 8-bit binary counter.

Solving for the input sequence

To recover the acceptance condition, we symbolically derive the flop's next state algebraically rather than by simulation. Starting at the D pin, we walk the netlist backwards, substituting each cell's liberty function for the cell it crosses and stopping at flop outputs and top-level inputs. Converting that function to disjunctive normal form tells us whether a flop must be 1 or 0. For success, this ends up being 25 literals that must be 1 and 29 that must be 0. Now that we have the structure that satisfies success, what's left is to search for the correct input that turns the success output high. hub only becomes a visible input to success's own D formula one cycle after it fires, so the unroll needs 121 cycles, not the 120 the counter period alone would suggest. While we were able to brute-force the warmup by exhaustive enumeration, that option isn't available here as the input space is 2121 compared to the 216 in the warmup.

Before handing this off to a solver, we build a cycle-accurate simulator in Python over the same netlist. The simulator reuses the same structures and backwards walk, but evaluates cells with real booleans instead of sympy expressions. The simulator is verified against the 312-posedge trace in example_inputs.vcd to ensure our parsed netlist from the symbolic derivation is valid.

Our solver is the Z3 Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) solver for Bounded Model Checking (BMC). The benefit of BMC here is that it removes the time dimension of our sequential circuit by making 121 copies of the circuit and wiring copy t's flop outputs into copy t + 1's flop inputs, essentially unrolling it into a combinational one. Based off our symbolic derivation, we have our bounding conditions: hub = 1, these 25 accumulator bits = 1, these 29 = 0. With our fully unrolled circuit, this becomes a boolean satisfiability problem. The full unroll built in about three seconds and solved in under one. When Z3 returns SAT, it also returns a model containing the values for I0, I1, I2… I120 as well as the value for every internal net and every flop at each of the 121 cycles, effectively returning a complete snapshot of a valid execution of the circuit. The complete snapshot is derivable by simulation making it redundant, but is occasionally useful for debugging, allowing us to inspect what the model claims a specific bit was doing at cycle 24 and compare against a concrete simulator. What's important here is we successfully retrieved a 121-bit input sequence that flips success high.

Solution: The hidden message

To verify our input sequence and extract the hidden message, we drive the input sequence into the extracted netlist under Icarus Verilog against our Skywater130 gate models. In the Icarus Verilog simulator, once success goes high, the 8-bit out begins streaming out 8-bit ASCII, one character per cycle. What we get is 28 2A 20 54 57 4F 20 53 54 41 52 53 20 2A 29 or our hidden message: (* TWO STARS *). After outputting those 15 consecutive bytes, out emits 0x00 from that cycle onwards and never restarts.

The 121-bit input sequence used to solve the puzzle turns out not to be an arbitrary key. Folded row-major into an 11×11 grid, it is actually a solved Star Battle board. The base-11 counter is what supplies the coordinates for each tile: at cycle i the design is looking at column i mod 11 of row i div 11, so each cycle addresses exactly one cell and the "press" represents a star being placed. The 54 flops of the acceptance condition turn out to be the Star Battle rules encoded into the circuit. 11 2-bit saturating counters, one per column, must each read exactly 2, and another 11, one per region, must do the same. _inst_346_ raises a flag on any sweep not containing exactly two presses, which enforces the two stars per row rule. And _inst_23_, fed by the 12-stage shift register, raises a flag whenever two stars touch horizontally, vertically or diagonally, since in a row-major scan, a cell's neighbours sit 1, 10, 11 and 12 cycles behind it. The 8-bit press counter reading 22 is a redundant check over the whole puzzle. Regions are the one piece of the puzzle not derivable from the counter. Since region membership is hardcoded in the combinational logic, we can instead check it by pressing each of the 121 cells individually and watching which region bit sets. We can verify this and see that the sizes of each contiguous region, ranging from 4 to 28 cells, when added up, sum to 121.
